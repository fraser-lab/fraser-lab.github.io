I like to take an active role in the preparation of all our manuscripts and love to help people clarify their writing for fellowships and job applications. Many learners are often afraid to show PIs anything other than finished work. This is a mistake! I expect a draft to be very rough and a starting point to grow from. If you think it's time to start a paper, let's get in a room and start hammering out an outline!

The first piece of a paper that I like to get done is the results headings. The best way to start a paper with me is to come in with some initial draft of these results headings. I'm also happy to work with people to come up with those header sentences.

## General workflow

It is variable, but this is the general workflow: we work on the outline and results first, then the introduction and discussion, and then later finish up the rest and edit. The final thing to do is to send to co-authors.

### 1. Outline and results

An outline is the headings of the different sections of the results as simple sentences that explain what was done and what the conclusion was, and ideally the figures that go along with them. Each results section follows the formula: “To explore [the thing in question], we [technique],” “Based on this, we understood X, and that suggested Y as a next step.” The next paragraph begins “To explore Y, we did technique Z,” and so on.
I care a lot about having clarity on what the topic sentence and the concluding sentence are. Then we write the middle bit at the same time as we iterate on the figure for the section. When I edit, I'm constantly looking for opportunities to reorient the text around the figures.

Once we're agreed on the results headers, it creates modules that can be moved around for storytelling, to make it much more comprehensible how the evidence fits together. That often means breaking the chronology of how things were done. After that, it's iterating on the results section over and over again.

### 2. Introduction and discussion

I usually write the results, then the abstract, discussion, and introduction. Once you have the results ironed out, it's a lot easier to write the introduction and the discussion, because you actually know what to introduce and what to discuss.

The introduction follows the format:

* Here is the field.
* Here is the gap that this paper is trying to address and why it hasn't been done before.
* Here is a summary of what we noticed in the literature that relates to the gap.
* The “here we show” paragraph.

The discussion is the reverse of that:

* Here we showed: [major conclusions].
* Here are the threads we think are still unresolved.
* Here are the major limitations (you can swap the order and do the limitations before that).
* Here's where we think this is going. This speculative section is the most important part of any discussion.

### 3. Finish up the rest and edit

At this stage, I generally expect the first author of the paper to write a full draft and decide on the details. I'll then iterate with them by reading the draft and provide feedback where we need more detail. Sometimes one-on-ones fit in with the writing and editing schedule, but expect to do one-off meetings to babysit the paper to its finish, which may involve meeting in the early morning or at 4 or 5pm. The final editing push will span several of these meetings.

### 4. Send to co-authors

Get feedback from co-authors, make final edits, post the preprint, and submit to a journal if applicable. Then celebrate!

## Tools and style

I highly prefer to write in Google Docs. I like to use the Document outline features, [Cross Reference](https://workspace.google.com/marketplace/app/cross_reference/269114033347){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} for figures and tables, and [Paperpile](https://paperpile.com/app){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"} (importing by PMID so that metadata is consistent) for references. I like to be able to see the whole picture, but also to be able to move figure five to figure two in real time. I do that a lot; that's part of my process with these modules, so the cross referencing and the outline feature are very important. It's a pretty lightweight way of doing it: very fast for me to work with, and immediately legible to everybody. I do not care about tracked changes and suggestions. I just want people to directly edit; I get very distracted by tracked changes and suggestions, and I edit my own words as much as I edit everyone else's.

I prefer a pretty direct, first person plural writing style. I prefer active voice (“we saw this, and next we did this”) and shorter, simple sentences. I want our papers in the lab to be very, very easy to read.
